Helpology

An automated medication dispenser that keeps dosing on schedule and keeps caregivers connected, remotely.

Developed from a device concept patented by sponsor Mindy Minhchau Le (US 2025/0082546 A1), now in its third generation of Senior Design development.

Background

Helpology is an automated medication dispensing system designed to help caretakers manage medication adherence for patients who cannot reliably self-administer their own pills and non-pill medications, such as older adults with cognitive or visual impairment. The device combines a motorized carousel and a linear-actuator-driven housing compartment, built around a Raspberry Pi 4, to store and dispense medication on a locally held schedule, ensuring doses are never missed due to network outages, while an onboard camera records each dispensing event so a remote caretaker can visually verify that medication was retrieved. Event records and video are relayed through a Firebase cloud backend to an Android mobile application, through which the caretaker can remotely schedule medications, review event history, receive real-time notifications (including on a paired smartwatch), and send short recorded voice messages to the patient.

By distributing responsibility across a device layer, cloud layer, and mobile application layer, Helpology aims to reduce the burden of in-person caregiving while preserving reliable, independent operation of the core medication-dispensing function. The system is based on a device concept patented by the project sponsor, developed from her own experience managing medication for a family member, and is now in its third generation of development across Senior Design teams.

Key Requirements

Conveyed directly by the sponsor from her own caregiving experience, this set of requirements addresses medication non-adherence, a leading cause of preventable harm among older adults, particularly those with cognitive or visual impairment who cannot reliably manage their own dosing schedules.

  • Carousel Verification: a sensor shall detect whether a dispensed dose remains in the cup or was removed.
  • Eye-Drop Compartments: compartments shall open when due, close after a timeout, and confirm bottle return, notifying the caregiver if not.
  • Event Recording: cameras shall record short motion-triggered clips (3–6 ft, 10–30 sec) rather than continuously, sufficient for a caregiver to review the dispensing event.
  • Caregiver–Patient Communication: the caregiver shall be able to speak to the patient in real time via the device’s speaker.
  • Patient Reminders: instructions shall be toggleable, escalating from a spoken prompt to a flashing light to an alarm if medication is not retrieved.
  • Video Retention: video shall retain for approximately 7 days by default, with caregiver view, save, and delete access; deleting a video shall not delete the associated medication event record, which remains available for later caregiver reporting.

Architectural Design

The architecture is bounded by specifications drawn from the System Requirements Specification and by constraints inherited from the project’s history and sponsorship. Helpology must begin each locally stored medication event within five seconds of its scheduled time even during network loss, confirm compartment state changes within one second, position the carousel within ten seconds, and maintain the refrigerated compartment between 2 and 8 degrees Celsius, among other timing and safety requirements. These specifications are constrained by a fixed deadline, a budget, dependence on hardware availability, and sponsor approval of all major design decisions. The most significant constraint, however, is that Helpology is based on a patented design already in its third generation of Senior Design development; rather than a clean-sheet design, the team inherited a working mechanical prototype with known electrical and software reliability problems, which shaped what could be freely redesigned versus what had to be corrected within the existing patent.

Because the team inherited rather than originated the core hardware, initial analysis centered on identifying which parts of the existing design were reliable enough to keep and which needed to be replaced, alongside a review of comparable commercial products such as the MedaCube, Hero Medication Manager, and EllieGrid to confirm which capabilities justified continued investment. This identified the carousel and compartment mechanisms as sound but under-instrumented, the cloud and mobile layers as largely unbuilt, and the carried-over facial recognition concept as added latency and risk without a clear safety benefit, leading the team to weigh sensor-only versus camera-assisted position verification, a general-purpose cloud provider versus an integrated platform such as Firebase, multi-platform versus single-platform mobile development, and whether to retain facial recognition at all. The team down-selected toward the options best suited to the compressed one-semester timeline and the sponsor’s revised priorities: camera-based caretaker verification was chosen over sensor-only confirmation because it satisfies the requirement that unconfirmed events never be reported as successful while reducing dependence on precise mechanical calibration in hardware with a history of reliability issues, with the existing encoder retained for motor control to limit integration risk; Firebase was selected over a general-purpose cloud provider because its database, storage, authentication, and messaging services are integrated under one platform, reducing backend work before the December deadline; Android was selected as the sole mobile target, with smartwatch support limited to notification mirroring, due to the fact that the sponsor owns an Android device, and facial recognition was removed from scope at the sponsor’s direction in favor of the simpler, more dependable retrieval-video confirmation already required elsewhere in the design.

Implementation Details

Device layer is built on a Raspberry Pi 4 running Raspberry Pi OS, with a DC motor and quadrature encoder driving the medication carousel, a 3″ linear actuator and physical limit switch controlling the housing compartment door, and obstruction detection handled by timing how long the actuator takes to fully extend. A USB camera with an embedded speaker/mic captures retrieval video and carries live caretaker audio, since the carousel’s position is now confirmed by caretaker video review rather than sensor feedback alone. A radiator-and-fan assembly, regulated through a thermistor and ADC reading into PWM fan control, keeps the refrigerated compartment within its target range, while a hand-soldered proto board driven through an I2C GPIO expander lights the compartment indicator LEDs, and a 7x3″ touchscreen serves as the onboard interface. Scheduling, actuator control, temperature regulation, and event logging run in a mix of Python and C on the Pi, with the time-critical motor and actuator control loops written in C for tighter timing than Python alone could reliably guarantee; all of this persists to a local SQLite database so medication is never missed during a network outage. Captured video is encoded to MP4 and uploaded over HTTPS, with pending uploads buffered to USB storage if the cloud is unreachable and retransmitted once connectivity returns. On the backend, Firebase (Cloud Functions, Firestore, Storage, and Cloud Messaging) authenticates the device and app via API keys, routes video and event records into durable storage, retains video for roughly seven days, and pushes formatted notifications outward. An Android app lets caregivers schedule medication, review event history, play back retrieval video, and send short recorded voice messages to the patient, caching recent data locally so the app stays usable when offline, with notifications mirrored to a paired smartwatch through standard phone-to-watch pairing rather than a separate watch application. Together, these layers were chosen specifically to keep the device’s core medication-dispensing function working independently of network conditions, backend availability, or app connectivity.

Conclusions

Helpology’s design closely matches the sponsor’s goals: reliable scheduled dispensing with confirmed retrieval, plus remote caregiver visibility. Our team’s main contribution was improving reliability left incomplete by prior teams — camera-verified retrieval, a consolidated Firebase backend, and a working Android app. The scope was narrowed with sponsor approval to prioritize reliability within the budget and timeline, a tradeoff we believe strengthens the final product even as it leaves room for future expansion. The result is a system the sponsor can confidently build on toward her long-term FDA submission goal. More broadly, this project reinforced how much engineering judgment goes into deciding what not to build, not just what to build. Thank you to our sponsor, Mindy Le, and the prior Senior Design teams who built the foundation we advanced.
